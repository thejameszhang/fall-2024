\begin{proof}
  $H$ is a nonempty subset of $Z$ that immediately contains $0, 2^{50},$ and $3^{50}$. 
  By the One-Step Subgroup Test, $H$ is a subgroup if $\forall \ a, b \in H, ab^{-1} \in H$. Additionally, 
  by the Two-Step Subgroup Test, $H$ is a subgroup if $\forall \ a, b, c \in H, ab \in H$ and $c^{-1} \in H$.
  Therefore, all linear combinations of $2^{50}$ and $3^{50}$ are also in $H$. To formalize this, 
  \[H = \{x2^{50} + y3^{50} \ | \ x, y \in \ZZ\}\]
  Considering the trivial prime factor decomposition of $2^{50}$ and $3^{50}$, it is 
  easy to see that they share no prime factors, so their GCD is 1, and they are relatively prime. Therefore, $1$ must be in $H$, and as 
  a consequence of Bezout's Identity,
  for any integer $z \in \ZZ$, we can find $x, y \in \ZZ$ such that $z = x2^{50} + y3^{50}$. 
  Therefore, $H$ must contain all integers, so $H$ is equal to $Z$. 
\end{proof}

\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
  
  First let us prove that the set $\langle a, b\rangle$ is a subgroup of $H$. We can prove 
  this by satisfying the conditions for either the One-Step Subgroup Test or the Two-Step Subgroup Test
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item For any $a, b \in H$, since $a^ib^j$ is also in $H$ for all $i, j \in \ZZ$, if we set $i = 1, b = -1$, then $ab^{-1}$ must be in $H$, 
    and so $H$ must be a subgroup of $G$.
    \item For any $a, b \in H$, since $a^ib^j$ is also in $H$ for all $i, j \in \ZZ$, $ab$ must also be in $H$ by setting $i = 1, j = 1$ 
    and $a^{-1}$ must also be in $H$ by setting $i = -1, b = 0$. Therefore, $H$ is a subgroup of $G$.
  \end{enumerate}
  Now let us describe $|\langle a, b \rangle|$ in terms of $|a|$ and $|b|$. Suppose $|a| = x, |b| = y$ and $x, y \in \NN + \{0\}$. 
  We're trying to find the minimum $n \in \NN + \{0\}$ such that 
  \[|\langle a, b \rangle| = |a^i b^j| = (a^ib^j)^n = a^{in}b^{jn} = e\] 
  For $a^{in} b^{jn} = 1$, we need $in$ to be a multiple of $x$ and $jn$ to be a multiple of $y$ so that $a^{in} = e$ and $b^{ij} = e$. Therefore, let 
  \begin{align*}
    n_1 = \frac{LCM(i, x)}{i} && n_2 = \frac{LCM(j, y)}{j}\\
  \end{align*}
  Thus we've found $n_1$ and $n_2$ such that $a^{in_1} = e$ and $b^{jn_2} = e$. To find the minimum $n$ such that both of these are true, simultaneously, let
  \[n = LCM(n_1, n_2) = LCM\left( \frac{LCM(i, |a|)}{i}, \frac{LCM(j, |b|)}{j}\right)\]
  and so we've expressed $|\langle a, b\rangle|$ in terms of $|a|$ and $|b|$, as desired.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
  Let $G$ be an infinite group, and let $a$ be any element in $G$. We will show that $\langle a \rangle$ is a subgroup of $G$, 
  and therefore, since there are an infinite number of elements in $G$, this will show that there are an infinite number of subgroups of $G$. 
  Since $a \in \langle a \rangle, \langle a \rangle$ is not empty. Let $a^n, a^m \in \langle a \rangle, n, m \in \ZZ$. Then,
  \[a^n(a^m)^{-1} = a^{n-m} = a^na^{-m} \in \langle a \rangle\]
  Therefore, by the One-Step Subgroup Test, $\langle a \rangle$ is a subgroup of $G$. Therefore, 
  there are an infinite number of subgroups of $G$, as desired.  
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
  Let $a, b \in G$. $|a| = 12 \implies a^{12} = e$ and $|b| = 22 \implies b^{22} = e$. Since 
  $\langle a \rangle \cap \langle b \rangle \neq \{e\}$, 
  \[\exists \ x \in \langle a \rangle \cap \langle b \rangle \text{ s.t. } x = a^i = b^j\]
  Note that the order of $x$ must divide $12$ and $22$. The divisors of $12$ and $22$ are $1$ and $2$. 
  We do not seek $1$ because if the order of $x$ is $1$, then $x = e$, but we pre-specified that $x \neq e$. Therefore, 
  the order of $x$ must be $2 \implies |x| = 2 \implies x^2 = e$. Therefore, 
  since $x = a^i = b^j$, 
  \[x^2 = a^{2i} = b^{2j} = e \implies i = 6, b = 11\]
  because $a^{12} = e = b^{22}$. Thus,
  \[(a^6)^2 = (b^{11})^2 \implies a^6 = b^{11}\]
  as desired.
\end{proof}
