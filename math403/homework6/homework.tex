\begin{proof}
  By definition of factor group, 
  \[Z_n \slash \langle k \rangle = \{a + \langle k \rangle \ | \ a \in Z_n\} = \{0 + \langle k \rangle, 1 + \langle k \rangle, \ldots, (n-1) + \langle k \rangle\}\]
  where recall that $Z_n = \{0, 1, \ldots, n-1\}$ and the subgroup $\langle k \rangle = \{0, k, 2k, \ldots (m-1)k\}$
  where $m = \frac{n}{k}$.
  However, observe the last term $(n-1) + \langle k \rangle$. Since $k$ is a divisor of $n$, the multiples of $k$ will cycle through the elements of $Z_n$ every $k$ steps. Thus, the cosets in the factor group are 
  \[Z_n \slash \langle k \rangle = \{0 + \langle k \rangle, \ldots, (k-1) + \langle k \rangle\}\]
  Thus, the factor group contains exactly $k$ distinct cosets, which is the same order as $Z_k$. 
  Thus, let us define the mapping $\phi: Z_n \slash \langle k \rangle \to Z_k$ where for all cosets $a + \langle k \rangle \in Z_n \slash \langle k \rangle$, 
  $\phi(a + \langle k \rangle) = a \text{ mod } k$. Note that this mapping is clearly 1-1 and onto because each element in $Z_k$ corresponds to exactly one coset in 
  $Z_n \slash \langle k \rangle$. Furthermore, $\phi$ is operation preserving because 
  $\phi((a + b) + \langle k \rangle) = \phi(a + \langle k \rangle) + \phi(a + \langle k \rangle)$, and 
  thus $Z_n \slash \langle k \rangle$ is isomorphic to $Z_k$.
\end{proof}

  \[Q \slash Z = \{a + Z \ | \ a \in Q\}\]

\begin{proof}
  Note that the group of integers under addition is an infinite, cyclic, Abelian group. 
  We will show that there does not exist a group $G$ such that $G \slash Z(G)$ is isomorphic to the integers 
  under addition. Recall two important properties of isomorphisms acting on groups: suppose 
  $G$ is isomorphic to $G'$, then $G$ is Abelian if and only if $G'$ is Abelian 
  and $G$ is cyclic if and only if $G'$ is cyclic. 

  \hfill

  We will proceed with contradiction. Assume on the contrary that $G \slash Z(G)$ is isomorphic to $Z$ under addition. 
  This implies that $G \slash Z(G)$ is Abelian, but if $G$ is non-Abelian, then the 
  quotient group to its center cannot be Abelian, and so we have reached a contradiction because a non-Abelian group cannot be 
  isomorphic to an Abelian group. If $G$ is Abeilian, then $G \slash Z(G)$ would be 
  be of order $1$ and isomorphic to the identity, which cannot be $Z$. Thus, there does not exist a group such that 
  $G \slash Z(G) \approx Z$ under addition. 
\end{proof}
